(scm data): joining and reshaping

The first data chapter covers frames and the basic verbs. This one combines tables and changes their shape.

(import (scheme base) (scheme write) (scm data))

(define orders
  (tibble (order 1 2 3 4)
          (customer "ada" "alan" "ada" "grace")
          (tea "Sencha" "Assam" "Oolong" "Sencha")
          (kg 2 1 1 3)))

(define customers
  (tibble (customer "ada" "alan" "edsger")
          (city "London" "Manchester" "Austin")))

Joins

A join adds the columns of a second table to the rows of the first that match on a key. left-join keeps every row of the first table; where there is no match the new columns are na:

(df-print (left-join orders customers (by customer)))
;; prints: # A data frame: 4 × 5
;; prints:   order  customer  tea        kg  city
;; prints:   <int>  <chr>     <chr>   <int>  <chr>
;; prints: 1     1  ada       Sencha      2  London
;; prints: 2     2  alan      Assam       1  Manchester
;; prints: 3     3  ada       Oolong      1  London
;; prints: 4     4  grace     Sencha      3  NA

The other joins differ in which rows they keep:

joinkeeps
left-joinevery row of the first table
inner-joinrows with a match in both
full-joinevery row of both
semi-joinrows of the first that have a match, without adding columns
anti-joinrows of the first that have no match
 
(df-nrow (inner-join orders customers (by customer))) ;; => 3
(df-nrow (full-join orders customers (by customer)))  ;; => 5
(df-column (semi-join customers orders (by customer)) 'customer) ;; => #("ada" "alan")
(df-column (anti-join customers orders (by customer)) 'customer) ;; => #("edsger")
(df-column (anti-join orders customers (by customer)) 'customer) ;; => #("grace")

The anti-joins answer the questions data checks ask: which customers never ordered, which orders name a customer we do not know.

When the key has different names in the two tables, pair them up; with several keys, list them all:

(define prices (tibble (name "Sencha" "Assam") (chf 48 22)))
(define (show-na v) (vector-map (lambda (x) (if (na? x) 'missing x)) v))
(show-na (df-column (left-join orders prices (by (tea . name))) 'chf)) ;; => #(48 22 missing 48)

(by shop year) joins on two columns. If a key occurs several times in the second table, the row of the first is repeated for each match, as in SQL: check keys that should be unique with tally first.

Stacking

bind-rows puts frames under each other, matching columns by name; a column missing in one of them is na there. bind-cols puts them side by side:

(df-print (bind-rows (tibble (tea "Sencha") (kg 2))
                     (tibble (tea "Assam") (shop "Bern"))))
;; prints: # A data frame: 2 × 3
;; prints:   tea        kg  shop
;; prints:   <chr>   <int>  <chr>
;; prints: 1 Sencha      2  NA
;; prints: 2 Assam      NA  Bern

Long and wide

The same numbers can be laid out wide, one column per month, as people like to read them, or long, one row per measurement, as the verbs and charts like to work with them:

(define monthly (tibble (tea "Sencha" "Assam") (jan 10 4) (feb 12 na) (mar 9 6)))

(define long (pivot-longer monthly (cols jan feb mar) (names-to month) (values-to kg)))
(df-print long)
;; prints: # A data frame: 6 × 3
;; prints:   tea     month     kg
;; prints:   <chr>   <sym>  <int>
;; prints: 1 Sencha  jan       10
;; prints: 2 Sencha  feb       12
;; prints: 3 Sencha  mar        9
;; prints: 4 Assam   jan        4
;; prints: 5 Assam   feb       NA
;; prints: 6 Assam   mar        6

The old column names become values of month (as symbols, since column names are symbols). pivot-wider goes back:

(df-print (pivot-wider long (names-from month) (values-from kg)))
;; prints: # A data frame: 2 × 4
;; prints:   tea       jan    feb    mar
;; prints:   <chr>   <int>  <int>  <int>
;; prints: 1 Sencha     10     12      9
;; prints: 2 Assam       4     NA      6

A typical round trip: summarise in long form, then widen for the report table, with (values-fill 0) for combinations that do not occur:

(df-print (-> orders
              (group-by tea customer)
              (summarise (kg (sum kg)))
              (pivot-wider (names-from customer) (values-from kg) (values-fill 0))))
;; prints: # A data frame: 3 × 4
;; prints:   tea      alan    ada  grace
;; prints:   <chr>   <int>  <int>  <int>
;; prints: 1 Assam       1      0      0
;; prints: 2 Oolong      0      1      0
;; prints: 3 Sencha      0      2      3

The new columns come in the order their names first appear in the data; select puts them in another.

Lists in cells, text to split

A cell may hold a list. unnest-longer gives each element a row of its own, and separate splits a text column at a separator:

(df-print (unnest-longer (tibble (order 1 2) (teas '("Sencha" "Assam") '("Matcha"))) teas))
;; prints: # A data frame: 3 × 2
;; prints:   order  teas
;; prints:   <int>  <chr>
;; prints: 1     1  Sencha
;; prints: 2     1  Assam
;; prints: 3     2  Matcha

(df-print (separate (tibble (code "ZH-8001" "BE-3011")) code (into canton plz) (sep "-")))
;; prints: # A data frame: 2 × 2
;; prints:   canton  plz
;; prints:   <chr>   <chr>
;; prints: 1 ZH      8001
;; prints: 2 BE      3011

The parts are strings; (mutate (plz (->number plz))) converts one that holds numbers.

Missing values

drop-na drops rows with a missing value (in the named columns, or in any), replace-na sets a value, and fill-down repeats the last value seen, which repairs spreadsheets where a group label is written only once:

(df-nrow (drop-na long kg))                 ;; => 5
(df-column (replace-na long (kg 0)) 'kg)    ;; => #(10 12 9 4 0 6)
(df-column (fill-down (tibble (shop "Zurich" na na "Bern" na) (kg 1 2 3 4 5)) shop) 'shop)
;; => #("Zurich" "Zurich" "Zurich" "Bern" "Bern")

Window functions

Window functions see the whole column (or group) but return a value per row: lag and lead (the previous and next value), cumsum, rank, share and others. Per group, they restart for each group:

(df-print (-> long
              (drop-na kg)
              (group-by tea)
              (mutate (change (- kg (lag kg))) (running (cumsum kg)) (place (rank (- kg))))))
;; prints: # A data frame: 5 × 6, groups: tea
;; prints:   tea     month     kg  change  running  place
;; prints:   <chr>   <sym>  <int>   <int>    <int>  <int>
;; prints: 1 Sencha  jan       10      NA       10      2
;; prints: 2 Sencha  feb       12       2       22      1
;; prints: 3 Sencha  mar        9      -3       31      3
;; prints: 4 Assam   jan        4      NA        4      2
;; prints: 5 Assam   mar        6       2       10      1

rank ranks from small to large; ranking the negated value puts the largest first. The first lag of each group is na, as there is no previous month. The rows must be in the right order for this: arrange first when they might not be.

Your own functions

define-df-function adds a function to the expression language. A scalar function works per row, an agg function reduces a group's values (missing ones already removed) to one:

(define-df-function 'with-vat 'scalar (lambda (chf) (* chf 1.081)))
(define-df-function 'spread 'agg (lambda (xs) (- (apply max xs) (apply min xs))))

(df-column (mutate prices (gross (round (with-vat chf)))) 'gross) ;; => #(52.0 24.0)
(df-print (-> long (group-by tea) (summarise (lowest (min kg)) (spread (spread kg)))))
;; prints: # A data frame: 2 × 3
;; prints:   tea     lowest  spread
;; prints:   <chr>    <int>   <int>
;; prints: 1 Assam        4       2
;; prints: 2 Sencha       9       3

A function used once is simpler as (,proc column); one that many analyses share is worth a name.

Splitting by group

df-split turns a grouped frame into one frame per group, for example to write one file per customer:

(map (lambda (part) (list (car part) (df-nrow (cdr part))))
     (df-split (group-by orders customer)))
;; => ((("ada") 2) (("alan") 1) (("grace") 1))

Each part is the group's key values and its frame.
