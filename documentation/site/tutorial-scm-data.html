(scm data): data frames and verbs

(scm data) is a toolkit for analysis scripts in the style of R's tidyverse and pandas: read a table, transform it, summarise it, chart it, write a report. This chapter covers the table itself and the verbs that transform it. Joins and reshaping, statistics, tests, charts and reports have chapters of their own (see the end).

(import (scheme base) (scheme write) (scm data))

One import brings all the (scm data …) libraries.

A data frame

A data frame is a table of named columns of equal length. tibble makes one column by column, and df-print shows it with the column types:

(define teas
  (tibble (tea "Sencha" "Assam" "Matcha" "Rooibos")
          (origin "Japan" "India" "Japan" "South Africa")
          (price 48 22 160 18)))

(df-print teas)
;; prints: # A data frame: 4 × 3
;; prints:   tea      origin        price
;; prints:   <chr>    <chr>         <int>
;; prints: 1 Sencha   Japan            48
;; prints: 2 Assam    India            22
;; prints: 3 Matcha   Japan           160
;; prints: 4 Rooibos  South Africa     18

(df-names teas)                             ;; => (tea origin price)
(df-nrow teas)                              ;; => 4
(df-column teas 'price)                     ;; => #(48 22 160 18)
(df-row teas 2)                             ;; => ((tea . "Matcha") (origin . "Japan") (price . 160))

Columns are vectors, rows come out as alists. A frame never changes: every operation returns a new one (sharing the columns it did not touch), so a frame can be passed around without copying.

alists->df builds a frame from rows, the shape data often arrives in:

(df-nrow (alists->df '(((tea . "Gyokuro") (price . 95)) ((tea . "Bancha") (price . 14))))) ;; => 2

Reading CSV

Real data comes from files. read-csv reads a file, read-csv-string text; the column types are inferred:

(define sales
  (read-csv-string
   "date,shop,tea,kg,plz
2025-03-03,Zurich,Sencha,2.5,8001
2025-03-03,Bern,Assam,1,3011
2025-03-04,Zurich,Assam,4,8001
2025-03-04,Basel,Sencha,1.5,4051
2025-03-05,Bern,Rooibos,3,3011
2025-03-05,Zurich,Matcha,0.5,8001
2025-03-06,Basel,Assam,,4051
2025-03-07,Zurich,Sencha,2,8001
2025-03-07,Bern,Sencha,1,3011
"))

(df-types sales)                            ;; => ((date . date) (shop . string) (tea . string) (kg . real) (plz . int))
  • ISO dates become dates, numbers become integers or reals.
  • The empty kg on 6 March is a missing value, na.
  • A value with a leading zero ("08001") would stay a string, so codes do not lose their zeros. 'col-types '((plz . string)) sets a type explicitly; postcodes are codes, not quantities.

read-csv takes 'sep #\; and 'decimal #\, for the European variant of CSV. The I/O chapter has the other formats.

Missing values

na is a value of its own. In the expressions of the verbs (below), arithmetic and comparisons with it give na; summaries skip it:

(na? (vector-ref (df-column sales 'kg) 6)) ;; => #t
(sum (df-column sales 'kg))                ;; => 15.5
(mean (list 1 2 na 3))                     ;; => 2.0
(na? (vector-ref (df-column (mutate sales (grams (* 1000 kg))) 'grams) 6)) ;; => #t

Outside the verbs na is an ordinary Scheme value: (+ 1 na) is an error, so test with na? first.

Rows: where, arrange, head

where keeps the rows for which a condition holds. Inside a verb a bare name is a column:

(df-print (where sales (= shop "Zurich") (>= kg 2)))
;; prints: # A data frame: 3 × 5
;; prints:   date        shop    tea        kg    plz
;; prints:   <date>      <chr>   <chr>   <dbl>  <int>
;; prints: 1 2025-03-03  Zurich  Sencha    2.5   8001
;; prints: 2 2025-03-04  Zurich  Assam       4   8001
;; prints: 3 2025-03-07  Zurich  Sencha      2   8001

Several conditions must all hold. A row whose condition is na (the missing kg) is dropped. arrange sorts, desc reverses a key, and head takes the first rows:

(df-print (head (arrange sales (desc kg)) 3))
;; prints: # A data frame: 3 × 5
;; prints:   date        shop    tea         kg    plz
;; prints:   <date>      <chr>   <chr>    <dbl>  <int>
;; prints: 1 2025-03-04  Zurich  Assam        4   8001
;; prints: 2 2025-03-05  Bern    Rooibos      3   3011
;; prints: 3 2025-03-03  Zurich  Sencha     2.5   8001

slice, tail, distinct and sample-n are the other row verbs.

Columns: select, mutate, rename

(define with-price (left-join sales teas (by tea)))

(df-print (-> with-price
              (mutate (revenue (* kg price)))
              (select date shop tea revenue)
              (head 3)))
;; prints: # A data frame: 3 × 4
;; prints:   date        shop    tea     revenue
;; prints:   <date>      <chr>   <chr>     <dbl>
;; prints: 1 2025-03-03  Zurich  Sencha      120
;; prints: 2 2025-03-03  Bern    Assam        22
;; prints: 3 2025-03-04  Zurich  Assam        88

left-join added the price of each tea from the first table (more on joins in the next chapter). mutate computes a column per row, select keeps columns in the given order. -> threads the frame through the steps: each step gets the result of the one before as its first argument. Read it as "then".

Groups: group-by and summarise

summarise reduces rows to one per group:

(df-print (-> with-price
              (mutate (revenue (* kg price)))
              (group-by shop)
              (summarise (sales (n)) (kg (sum kg)) (revenue (sum revenue)))
              (arrange (desc revenue))))
;; prints: # A data frame: 3 × 4
;; prints:   shop    sales     kg  revenue
;; prints:   <chr>   <int>  <dbl>    <dbl>
;; prints: 1 Zurich      4      9      384
;; prints: 2 Bern        3      5      124
;; prints: 3 Basel       2    1.5       72

(n) counts the rows of the group. tally is the short form of counting alone:

(df-print (tally (group-by sales tea)))
;; prints: # A data frame: 4 × 2
;; prints:   tea          n
;; prints:   <chr>    <int>
;; prints: 1 Assam        3
;; prints: 2 Matcha       1
;; prints: 3 Rooibos      1
;; prints: 4 Sencha       4

On a grouped frame mutate computes per group, so aggregates refer to the group, for example each sale's share of its shop's kilograms, in per cent:

(df-column (-> sales (drop-na kg) (group-by shop) (mutate (pct (round (* 100 (/ kg (sum kg)))))))
           'pct)
;; => #(28.0 20.0 44.0 100.0 60.0 6.0 22.0 20.0)

The expression language

The expressions inside verbs are not ordinary Scheme: they are compiled once and then run per row, with bare names standing for columns. The vocabulary has arithmetic and comparisons, and/or/not, if, case-when, in, coalesce, string functions (str-upper, str-detect, str-sub …), date functions (year, weekday, days-between …), aggregates (n sum mean median sd min max …) and window functions (lag cumsum rank …):

(df-print (-> sales
              (mutate (size (case-when ((< kg 1) 'small) ((< kg 3) 'medium) (else 'large)))
                      (day (weekday date)))
              (where (in tea ("Sencha" "Matcha")))
              (select tea kg size day)))
;; prints: # A data frame: 5 × 4
;; prints:   tea        kg  size      day
;; prints:   <chr>   <dbl>  <sym>   <int>
;; prints: 1 Sencha    2.5  medium      1
;; prints: 2 Sencha    1.5  medium      2
;; prints: 3 Matcha    0.5  small       3
;; prints: 4 Sencha      2  medium      5
;; prints: 5 Sencha      1  medium      5

weekday counts Monday as 1. case-when takes the first branch whose condition is true; a missing value makes a condition false, so a row with no kg would land in else, as in R.

A Scheme value from outside goes in with unquote, and (,proc …) calls a Scheme procedure per row:

(define minimum 2)
(define (label shop kg) (string-append shop ": " (number->string kg) " kg"))

(df-column (-> sales (where (>= kg ,minimum)) (mutate (text (,label shop kg)))) 'text)
;; => #("Zurich: 2.5 kg" "Zurich: 4.0 kg" "Bern: 3.0 kg" "Zurich: 2.0 kg")

Without the comma, minimum would be taken for a column, and there is none of that name:

(where sales (>= kg minimum))               ;; => error

Dates

A date is a day number; the date functions work on it, and comparisons work as for numbers:

(define d (string->day "2025-03-05"))
(day->string d)                             ;; => "2025-03-05"
(day->ymd d)                                ;; => (2025 3 5)
(day->string (ymd->day 2025 12 24))         ;; => "2025-12-24"
(day->string (string->day "24.12.2025"))    ;; => "2025-12-24"

(df-nrow (where sales (>= date ,(string->day "2025-03-05")))) ;; => 5
(df-column (-> sales (mutate (age (days-between date ,(ymd->day 2025 3 7)))) (head 2)) 'age) ;; => #(4 4)

Example: what sold on which day

Raw sales rows to a weekday summary, the kind of table that ends up in a report: kilograms per weekday, the best-selling tea of each day, and the day's share of the week.

(define day-names #("Mon" "Tue" "Wed" "Thu" "Fri" "Sat" "Sun"))
(define (day-name n) (vector-ref day-names (- n 1)))

(define per-day
  (-> sales
      (drop-na kg)
      (mutate (day (,day-name (weekday date))))
      (group-by day tea)
      (summarise (kg (sum kg)))
      (group-by day)
      (mutate (best (= kg (max kg))))
      (where best)
      (ungroup)
      (select day tea kg)))

(df-print per-day)
;; prints: # A data frame: 4 × 3
;; prints:   day    tea         kg
;; prints:   <chr>  <chr>    <dbl>
;; prints: 1 Fri    Sencha       3
;; prints: 2 Mon    Sencha     2.5
;; prints: 3 Tue    Assam        4
;; prints: 4 Wed    Rooibos      3

The second group-by regroups the per-tea sums by day, so (max kg) is the maximum of the day, and where keeps that row. Thursday is missing: its only sale has no weight and was dropped. summarise sorts by its groups, which puts the days in alphabetical order; to get them in calendar order, keep the weekday number and sort by it.

Building verbs at run time

Each verb is a macro, and has a procedure behind it that takes the expressions as data: df-filter, df-mutate, df-summarise, df-arrange, df-select … They are for code that builds an analysis from a configuration or user input:

(define rules '((>= kg 2) (= shop "Zurich")))
(df-nrow (df-filter sales rules))           ;; => 3
(df-column (df-mutate (head sales 2) '((grams . (* 1000 kg)))) 'grams) ;; => #(2500.0 1000.0)
